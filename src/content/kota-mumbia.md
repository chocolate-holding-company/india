<p class="text-lead-fluid! leading-relaxed mt-6">Some journeys are about the destination. This one was about the <strong>sixteen hours in between</strong>. After a <strong>dodgy hotel</strong> in Kota, I swapped the comfort of first class for a <em>wooden luggage rack in a packed second-class carriage</em>, with my bicycle <strong>stitched into a sack</strong> somewhere down the train. Along the way came <strong>staring</strong> contests, <strong>chai sellers</strong>, a fan of <strong>ten-rupee</strong> notes, and one <strong>very lost</strong> ticket.</p>

---

As I reached the edge of Kota, I spotted a couple of hotels. One seemed pretty fancy, but the other looked like a great budget option—and being just 2 km from the centre made it even more inviting. I asked the young guy at the desk about a room, and he asked me to wait for the manager. The manager was out in a field handling some construction, so I hung around for a while. After about 15 minutes, I decided to head into the heart of town instead.

The town centre was a usual bustling, scruffy copy of Delhi—nothing looked painted or clean. I spotted what seemed like a potential hotel with a courtyard, although it looked like it was under-going a little construction, it was perfect for keeping my bike off the street. A big, sturdy guy stood outside; at first I thought he might be a guest, but it turns out he was probably part of the staff, likely handling security. The room cost roughly 400 rupees and was small but sufficient for one night.

## Room Service Nobody Ordered

Shortly after I settled in, a knock came at the door; two room boys asked whether I required anything. One spoke clear English, while the other was a clumsy youth who attempted English but produced only gibberish. Ultimately, I requested juice, handed them 15 rupees, and they departed. Ten minutes later they returned with the juice; I thanked them, though they lingered as usual, waiting for a tip, _they would have made money on purchasing the juice_. They finally departed but came back after ten minutes to ask if I needed anything else; I kept saying no, so they left again.

A short time later they reappeared, this time three of them, with a young lad joining the group. They asked once more if I required anything, and when I declined, the foolish one tried to enter the room; I stopped him and pushed him back gently. The English speaker kept asking if I needed anything; he was fine and always spoke politely. They continued to hassle me, and the foolish one kept ranting in Indian, growing quite excited. By now I had had enough and told them to leave.

Finally they did, only to knock on the door again five minutes later. The three were still there, and the English speaker apologised for the foolish one, who had clearly knocked. The foolish one asked for 50 rupees; I said no, but he persisted and tried to force his way in to the room. I pushed him back, yet he kept advancing and shouting **50 rupees**. The lad simply watched while the English speaker tried to console the foolish one. He kept pushing and shouting 50 rupees, so this time when he tried to enter, I placed my hand on his chest to stop him and push him; he went back with little force, as there was nothing on him. The English speaker intervened, saying ‘_no problem, no problem_’; I told him there was a problem and that I would throw this man over the balcony if I saw him again—we were on the first floor overlooking the small courtyard and building site where Id left the bike. Finally I let go and they left, the foolish one still ranting and raving. I thought I might receive a visit from the big guy at the front, but I never saw any of them again, not even when I checked out the next day; they usually line up for service tips.

## Queueing, Indian Style (Elbows Encouraged)

I made my way down to the train station, located the booking hall, and waited in line while trying to hold my spot and watch the bike parked by a post inside the hall. In India, orderly queues are rare; people often push forward or head straight to the window. To prevent this, you must keep the line tight or block them, which usually sends them to another opening further up the queue. Indians tend to tolerate this behaviour and end up waiting ten times longer.

## One Ticket to Mumbai, and One Very Confused Clerk

When I finally reached the window, I requested a ticket to Mumbai for myself and the cycle. He looked at me with confusion and spoke words I could not grasp. His expression and posture suggested surprise at my presence buying a second-class ticket. After a pause, he sighed, agreed, and issued a ticket costing 185 rupees for a 16-hour journey. He told me to take the bike to the luggage department where they would arrange the cost.

## The Bike Gets Wrapped Like a Christmas Present

I found the luggage department, which was on the train platform and checked in the bike, completing a lengthy form that took around ten minutes. After paying 120 rupees for the bike, I wheeled it out where another person wrapped it in sack material and stitched it so only the wheels remained visible. A second worker requested the ticket and marked the destination, ticket number, and additional details on the sack in Hindi using white paint. This process took roughly 45 minutes and cost 50 rupees. I kept hold of the two panniers.

## Upgrade Denied: Sardines Only

A friendly young girl struck up a conversation with me in very good English on the platform and suggested upgrading my ticket. She asked the usual questions—_where I was from and where I was headed_. When I told her I was going to Mumbai, she mentioned it’s a 16-hour trip. She explained that second class could be pretty rough—crowded, uncomfortable, and maybe even risky—so she really encouraged me to upgrade. I’d already decided that I didn’t think second class was for me and went off to see about upgrading my ticket.

The guy at the upgrade desk said it wasn’t possible right then, but he advised me to come back an hour before the train was due to leave to check for open spots in first class, sleeper class, or AC class. I spent the time wandering around town or reading at the station. Watching the rats scurry along the tracks and disappear into holes under the station helped the time trickle by.

Finally, an hour before the train arrived, I headed to the upgrade office. “Not possible; the train is full,” said the man behind the counter. About twenty others were trying to upgrade. That settled it, I thought: I had two choices—let the bike continue to Mumbai and book another day for myself, or keep traveling second class. The idea of staying in Kota at least another day, even in a different hotel, lacked appeal, as did letting the bike carry on and fend for it’s self and anyway, second class would be an experience; I decided to continue and yes, it was an experience.

## All Aboard the Human Jigsaw Puzzle

The train arrived on time. It looked big and heavy with a **dirty diesel smell**. I could hardly believe what I saw: people were literally hanging out of the doors. I walked along the platform and inspected the carriages, which were packed like sardines. I watched a young lad push the bike toward the luggage carriage. I wondered whether he worked for the railway or was rushing for the entrance, but he seemed genuine enough.

Boarding any carriage seemed impossible, so I chose one and forced my way through the packed entrance. As I moved forward with two panniers trailing behind, I struck an older man seated on the floor and dragged them over his head. He and a younger lad, likely a relative, objected, but I had no choice; he should have stood to clear the narrow opening he had formed.

Looking down the carriage, I saw people everywhere—in the seats, on the luggage racks, and in the aisle. The aisle was roughly three feet wide with compartments on the right. Each compartment held long seats on both sides and a luggage rack above, the compartments divided by a wire grille. Every seat and rack above was filled with people pressed together. Another luggage rack ran along the top of the left hand side of the carriage, packed with bags and the occasional child curled in a foetal position between them. I stacked my panniers near the door, next to the smelly toilet and scanned the carriage for space, but could see none.

## Location, Location, Toilet

People kept pushing past some boarding the train and some heading to the single _hole in the floor toilet_, about five feet away from where I was standing; the odour was overwhelming. I desperately wanted to disembark and rebook a seat in tourist class. The prospect of standing here for sixteen hours felt unbearable, like hell. And then the train moved slowly, leaving me trapped at least until the next station.

Passengers continued to push past for the toilet, and the smell intensified. A queue formed, making passage difficult for those exiting, and I was pressed against the wall. The crowd eventually thinned, allowing me to breathe again. The train crept forward and gained speed until we cruised at roughly fifty miles per hour—as fast as it would go—so the journey would indeed be long. It was 2pm, and we were expected to reach Mumbai in the morning at 7am.

## The World Staring Championships

I sensed constant stares, and even when I met their gaze, they persisted. This seems normal in India, a kind of curious, hypnotic trance. I tried to break it by staring back, and they eventually looked away, only to resume once I turned my gaze. I could not challenge them all; trying to outstare everyone was too much, so I ignored it and sought a comfortable standing position, which was not easy.

## "Ten Rupee, Ten Rupee!": The Fan Dance

A chanting sound rose from a near compartment, coming from someone I can only call a begging figure in a sari. This person appeared before me, repeating a phrase that concluded with ten rupees. The individual had very short hair and unusually large earrings that seemed to strain the ears. I believe it was a man, wearing heavy eye makeup and bold red lipstick. The blue sari hung loosely, particularly around the flat chest. In his left hand, four ten-rupee notes were held between the fingers creating a fan and fanned continuously during the chant.

He moved toward me, leaving no escape; the notes were waved before my face while the other hand brushed my ear or cheek. “Ten rupee, ten rupee,” followed by another chant in Indian. I refused firmly and looked away, but this failed, so I said a resolute no while meeting the heavily made-up eyes. I pushed his hand aside when he reached for my ear or cheek, everyone was watching and laughing.

Eventually he gave up and repeated the act in the nearest compartment, where laughter turned to head shaking. The beggar persisted, chanting and touching facial features, giving them more attention than I got. Most grew uneasy and handed over ten rupees; some claimed they lacked that amount, so the beggar accepted whatever change they offered.

He seemed to exert a kind of influence that ordinary street beggars lacked. He appeared to bully the money from them as few resisted while he moved slowly through the carriage. It looked like a profitable hour, roughly the time needed to traverse the carriage. Then he likely jumped off at the next station and boarded the next carriage.

## Footnote Corner: A Quick Lesson in Hijra Culture

> [!Note] I later learned that the individual in question belonged to the Hijra community, frequently identified as Kinnar in northern India. Although Western observers may apply terms such as “transvestite” or “transgender,” Hijra denotes a distinct cultural and social identity with centuries of history in South Asia.`
>
> In 2014, the Supreme Court of India formally recognised Hijras as a legal “third gender.” Within traditional Hindu belief systems, Hijras are regarded as possessing spiritual authority to confer blessings—particularly for fertility and prosperity—or to impose curses. When they approach passengers on trains, typically signalling their presence with rhythmic hand-clapping, individuals provide monetary contributions for several reasons:
>
> - **Seeking blessings:** Some passengers pursue a blessing for good fortune.
> - **Avoiding curses:** Others offer small sums to avert perceived misfortune.
> - **Preventing confrontation:** Hijras may be persistent, occasionally employing public embarrassment or assertive behaviour to elicit contributions.
>
> Traveling for only one or two stops is a pragmatic tactic. It enables rapid movement through crowded carriages, collection of alms, and disembarkation before railway authorities or police can intervene, as unauthorised solicitation and begging on Indian railways are technically prohibited. They then wait at the station to board a train traveling in the opposite direction.`
>
> Historically, Hijras occupied respected and influential roles in royal courts, including during the Mughal period. In contemporary society, however, they encounter profound stigma, marginalisation, and violence. Many are estranged from their families at an early age and integrate into organised communes directed by a guru. Due to pervasive discrimination, they are largely excluded from mainstream education and formal employment. Consequently, most members of the community depend on traditional, culturally sanctioned means of subsistence: performing blessings at weddings and births (badhai), soliciting alms on streets and trains, or engaging in sex work.

## Promoted to the Luggage Rack

Soon after a young man began speaking to me while waiting in the toilet queue. He asked the usual questions: ‘_where are you from_’, ‘_what is your name_’, ‘_why are you in 2nd class_’? I explained I was there for the experience. He said ‘you cannot stand here for the whole journey; there is a spare place with me and my friends’.

I followed him down the carriage, stepping over people sitting in the aisle. The compartment was about halfway along the carriage and was packed with people, several older people were crowded together on one of the wooden seats. Opposite them sat a mix of younger people, again squeezed together on the other official train seat. Above them on one luggage rack was a man and two lads; on the opposite rack sat an older man with what appeared to be his son. The helpful lad climbed onto the rack next to the older man and invited me to sit beside him. The man and his son shuffled under protest to make valuable space for us. I placed my two panniers on the luggage rack that ran the whole length of the left side of the carriage, pushing a protesting lad into a tighter space. I climbed up; the rack had five bars spaced two inches apart and was quite uncomfortable. I removed my shoes, as everyone else seemed to have done, and set them on the safety cage of the nonworking fan. Fans ran the length of the carriage, but none functioned and all covered with shoes.

## Chai, Roti and Mystery Snacks

The journey was long and uncomfortable, with the main highlight occurring when we pulled into a station. Tea sellers swarmed the carriage windows, offering their wares—chai, spiced roti bread, and other snacks I didn’t recognise. Upon seeing me, they paused in disbelief and laughed: ‘_a foreigner in second class_’. They had little time to sell as much as possible-there were so many of them and the train didn’t stop for very long. The scene was frantic and very quick, too quick for me, and I didn’t have a clue what to ask for. I had made a major mistake by not buying food or drink for the trip, but the upside was that I might avoid the toilet—somewhere I dreaded I may have to visit. A couple of my travel companions gave me some fruit and biscuits; they were good, and I was grateful because these people had little to spare.

## Sixteen Hours of Not Sleeping

The journey passed slowly with little happening. We continued the staring game, which had become quite normal. Conversation faded as time passed-well, it required effort from both sides. It was noisy as you can imagine, a lot of people talking, nobody seemed to sleep. I nodded off a couple of times but only for second or two, it was just too uncomfortable.

## Thane: The Great Carriage Evacuation

Upon reaching **Thane**, about an hour before the final stop, Mumbai, the train emptied. Hundreds disembarked, leaving just me and a young couple in our carriage. I could hardly believe it, but apparently Thane serves as the main hub with links to all Mumbai suburbs.

I used this opportunity to catch a little sleep, stretching out on the official train seat. It was wooden, hard, and uncomfortable, so I knew sleep would be unlikely. I heard chanting as one of the _Hijra beggars_ moved through the carriage. No one was left to approach except the couple in the next compartment. I pretended to be asleep when the beggar came near; he reached the couple first and chanted his spells. They gave him his penance and said something that included the word tourist. I knew they meant me, and from what I could gather, they had given the beggar a little extra to leave me alone. The beggar turned and walked back the way he had come.

## Mumbai at Last (Minus One Ticket)

We arrived at the final station, by which time I had exchanged a few words with limited understanding with the couple who said they were visiting relatives in Mumbai. I prepared to leave the train, only to realise I lacked a ticket. Somehow I had lost it; I wonder what happens to _fare dodgers_ in India.

## Lost Ticket, Lost Bike, Lost in Translation

I went to collect the cycle from what I assumed was the luggage office. It was the luggage office, but not the place for larger items. I learned this after a lengthy exchange of mumbo-jumbo between me and the luggage man. After I showed him my cycle ticket, he indicated the cycle could be anywhere on the platform with the rest of the larger luggage. Then he asked for my train ticket-I said “_no train ticket_”, and he immediately became official, repeating several times “**no ticket**”, while I kept agreeing. In the end, he summoned me to walk with him along the platform to a shed-like office and motioned me to wait. He went inside and returned a few minutes later, motioning me to follow him into the office.

## Interrogation for Beginners

The office was small and windowless. I was asked to sit while one of two men questioned me in broken English. “You have no ticket,” he said; “that’s right-I have lost it.” He remarked, “that’s very careless of you,” and requested the cycle ticket and my passport. After recording details from both documents, he said very casually, “_they have put your cycle off at the wrong station_.” I wanted to say, “that’s very careless of you”-but thought I should avoid antagonising him before learning my fate for fare dodging. He said, “the cycle will be put on the next train-so I suggest you go and book your hotel and come back.” I had told him I wasn’t staying anywhere at the moment, as he needed that information for his document.

By the time I had arranged the hotel, which was not far from the station, it was early afternoon. I had arrived in Mumbai at about 8am, so I believed sufficient time had passed for the cycle to appear. It was about a 15-minute walk to the station, and it was hot. When I reached the shed-cum-office, I saw the bike leaning against a post, still wrapped in its over-the-top sacking.

## The 50-Rupee Certificate of Redemption

At the office, the man again mentioned the ‘lost ticket’—“_you will have to provide a certificate of redemption_,” he said. “How do I obtain one?” I asked. “Go into the main hall and locate the (_a man with a title I can’t remember)_; he will supply you with a ticket of redemption.” He noticed my blank expression, which likely prompted him to assign one of his colleagues to accompany me.

We moved toward the main hall. On entering the hall it was massive, with offices and shops occupying nearly all wall space except for a couple of large entrances. The hall resembled a large waiting room with benches everywhere, all occupied. Roughly a thousand people sat or stood around. Many seated men looked like clerks, each carrying similar hard briefcases. I expected my guide to enter an office, but he did not; instead, he scanned and scoured the hall. After searching the crowd for a couple of minutes, he recognised someone and we headed in his direction. He said something that led the man to open his briefcase and retrieve a pad. He completed a slip, handed it to me, and demanded 50 rupees. And that was it _—how simple_ **—what chance would I have had?**

## Back to the Chaos, Bike in Tow

After finishing the paperwork, I was allowed to take the cycle. A man removed the sacking, which seemed to take ages. I got the cycle outside and fought my way back to the hotel; it felt like being back in Delhi, with roads busy and horrendous. At the hotel, I had already mentioned having a cycle, but I was not permitted to keep it in the room. It was the usual arrangement: secure it outside and let the security guard watch it. At least their security guards are 24hr.
