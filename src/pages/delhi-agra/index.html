Lost in India: A Chronicle of a Delhi-to-Agra Cycling Adventure

Pahari? Of all the places to end up, I had somehow managed to cycle miles in the wrong direction. As the rain began to fall in earnest, bringing with it a biting chill and absolute darkness, I faced a daunting reality.

7 Aug 2026

Where! Pahari? A few words of my English finally got through, only to find that I had ended up in the wrong direction. The rain came down harder, it was cold and dark.

What now? I could have cried, the only way back was to retrace my steps back to Kaman and from there make my way to the Delhi-Agra highway. The thought of this was daunting, I was totally pissed off, what a stupid mistake I’d made.

Leaving Delhi and the Gurgaon Dust

My journey had begun promisingly enough the day before with a fine ride from Delhi. Navigating the labyrinth of Delhi’s roads, however, required a rather hands-on approach: I had to stop and ask directions from nearly every second person I encountered on the route to Gurgaon, a satellite city located roughly 21 kilometres away. Reassuring road signs only decided to make an appearance once I had finally reached the outer edges of the city outskirts.

The traffic itself was chaotic—a frantic press of commuters, each person seemingly in a desperate rush to push their way through the narrow gaps. Along the way, I had planned to visit the Qutub Minar, the spectacular ancient temple tower on Delhi's outskirts. Unfortunately, traveling with heavy luggage on a bicycle presents distinct practical challenges; the site staff would not permit me to bring my bicycle inside, and leaving it unattended with my gear was out of the question. Ultimately, I chose to bypass the monument and push onward to Gurgaon.

Gurgaon is a sprawling metropolis in the making, dominated by towering construction projects and a brand-new metro line designed to link it directly with Delhi. However, this rapid modernization meant that the roads were a massive, dry dust bowl. The roadworks were a marvel of manual labor: teams of women and children carried heavy loads of stone and sand balanced on their heads, depositing them for the men who raked the surface flat ahead of the steamrollers. Occasionally, a truck would dump a load of fresh tar to seal the road. For me and my bicycle, this meant navigating constant detours, pushing through rough, sandy tracks that left both of us entirely caked in fine red dust.

The Begging Priest of Sohna

By the time I rolled into Sohna, it was still too early in the day to check into a hotel, so I decided to continue toward Hodal. This was the exact moment my logistical plans collided with cosmic fate.

I was approached by a scruffy, gypsy-looking begging priest whose long black hair had clearly boycotted a comb for several years. He had highly unusual, penetrating blue eyes. Locking his gaze onto mine, he muttered a prayer, gestured toward the heavens, and placed his hand firmly on my head in a solemn blessing. Having finished his spiritual duties, he naturally extended his hand for a monetary reward.

Unaware of the local customs of cosmic billing, I shook my head and told him "no".

This turned out to be a grave error. Almost immediately after refusing his request, I missed a crucial junction and found myself cycling miles off-course on the road to Alwar. I became thoroughly convinced that my refusal had officially cursed my entire journey to Agra.

The Road to Alwar and the 'Blow Horn' Trucks

Despite the phantom curse, the ride to Alwar was actually quite pleasant—flat, straightforward, and passing through endless fields of potatoes. Every single square inch of arable land was being actively farmed. What surprised me most was the sheer abundance of water. Massive quantities were being pumped across the fields for irrigation, forming large ponds along the roadside. The local infrastructure served a dual purpose: women washed laundry while men took refreshing outdoor showers directly under the high-pressure water pumps in the blazing sun.

The traffic, however, remained wild. I was repeatedly forced off the paved road by the intimidating presence of "Blow Horn" trucks. These massive vehicles derive their nickname from the brightly painted, artistic lettering on their rears, which politely requests: "BLOW HORN OK PLEASE". Most of these trucks were held together by hope and rust; I passed several battered oil tankers with outer shells so riddled with holes that they would never have been deemed roadworthy in another country.

When a vehicle breaks down on an Indian highway, the mechanics are remarkably resourceful. They pull half-off the tarmac—rarely with much success—and erect a defensive wall of large stones around the exposed side of the truck. They then set to work performing heavy mechanical repairs right there in the active lane. The trucks may be falling apart, but they keep moving.

Night Riding and Paperwork in Alwar

By the time I finally reached the outskirts of Alwar, night had firmly set in. I was forced to navigate the final 12 kilometres in absolute darkness without a bicycle light—an incredibly nerve-wracking experience given the poor or non-existent street lighting.

Once in the city, I thought I had struck gold when I found a clean, inexpensive hotel. However, bureaucratic realities soon intervened: the receptionist did not possess the official registration forms required for foreign tourists, making it legally impossible for me to stay. Alwar is a sizeable city, and the clerk directed me to an alternative hotel located quite some distance away. Naturally, I got lost in the dark and stumbled into a completely different, rather scruffy hotel. Given that it was late and I had logged a gruelling 108-mile ride, I was simply glad to have a roof over my head.

Backtracking and the Friendly Local Guide

The following morning began with a predictable challenge: finding my way out of Alwar. The complete lack of directional signs in Indian towns is truly astonishing. Consequently, I had to backtrack 12 kilometres just to locate the road leading to Deeg. The sky was overcast and threatened rain, which actually made for much cooler, more comfortable riding conditions.

As I rode, I reflected on the previous evening in Alwar. A local youth had kindly guided me to a cheap, clean restaurant where I enjoyed a delicious meal and a cold beer. During the dinner, the waiter asked if the boy standing outside the window was with me; I explained he had merely pointed out the establishment. The next time I looked, the lad had made himself comfortable at a back table inside, quietly reading a newspaper. I assumed the restaurant was rewarding him for bringing in a customer.

However, when I paid my bill and left, the boy followed me. I thanked him and said goodbye, but he persisted, repeating: "I have been good to you, showed you good restaurant, good friend". Realising he was soliciting a guiding fee, I handed over 50 rupees. Far from satisfied, he immediately claimed his mother was in the hospital awaiting an urgent operation. When I made it clear that my wallet was officially closed, he departed in deep disappointment.

The Rainy Road to Deeg

The road to Deeg mirrored the previous day’s landscape—flat terrain slicing through endless agricultural fields. By mid-morning, a light drizzle had set in, escalating into a torrential downpour by afternoon. Because I had not anticipated such weather in India, I had failed to pack any waterproof gear. The dry road dust instantly transformed into thick, sticky mud that caked my clothes and my bicycle.

To add to the discomfort, I was constantly trailed by local youths on motorcycles and scooters. They asked the exact same series of questions on repeat: "How are you? What is your name? Which country?". By the end of the day, my patience had worn thin. Furthermore, obtaining directions was an exercise in linguistic precision: unless you pronounce the destination with theatrical accuracy, locals simply stare blankly. For instance, "hotel" must be pronounced precisely as "Otal" or "Otel," though they generally seem to comprehend "guest house" far better.

Seeking Help at Deeg Police Station

Desperate to escape the rain and secure accommodation in Deeg, I kept asking for a guest house, but my queries were met with blank stares. I eventually decided to seek help at the local police station. Once I finally managed to convey my request, the locals kindly pointed the way.

Inside the office, I was greeted by three officers. When I asked if there was a hotel or guest house in town, one of the officers launched into a lengthy, solemn speech implying that I had a major problem. I politely corrected him, explaining that I didn’t have a problem at all; I simply wanted information. Eventually, a plainclothes clerk took over the conversation. After a highly complex bilingual exchange, I finally learned the disappointing truth: Deeg’s sole hotel had closed down and been converted into a restaurant. Left with no other options, I had to pedal back out into the pouring rain.

Getting Lost on the Road to Pahari

I departed Deeg under the impression that I was on the highway to Mathura, but I missed a turn and was actually cycling in the exact opposite direction. Curious local motorcyclists continued to ride alongside me, shouting the familiar refrain of "Who? Where? What?". With the rain pouring and daylight fading rapidly, I finally rolled into Pahari.

At a small roadside kiosk, I showed my map to a local man. He squinted at the paper and pointed to our actual location, revealing that I was miles off-course. A curious crowd quickly gathered around us in the downpour. Seeing my drenched and miserable state, one of the men in the crowd took pity on me. He mentioned the word "hostel," which sounded like absolute music to my ears, and gestured for me to follow him to his home. Upon arrival, he escorted me into a small courtyard flanked by three modest buildings.

The Garden-Shed Temples of Pahari

There were two older men and three children gathered in the courtyard. It turned out that two of the buildings surrounding us were active temples—though, in truth, they bore an uncanny resemblance to garden sheds. One of these "sheds" was entirely filled by a massive statue of Shiva. Standing there drenched, I felt a slight twinge of guilt as I privately thought: "What an absolute waste of a perfectly good garden shed".

One of the elderly men was actively performing a service—banging drums, ringing bells, and chanting prayers aloud—while the rest of the family knelt on the wet ground, bowing and kissing the courtyard floor. I stood awkwardly to the side, unsure of the correct protocol, and waited for the ceremony to conclude. Afterward, I was politely offered some blessed "karli," a temple sweet that tasted remarkably like sugary cardboard.

Checking into the Temple Hostel

Once prayers concluded, a lively debate ensued regarding my accommodation. The older man who had conducted the prayers mentioned the word "hostel" once more, which greatly relieved me. I was escorted to the main temple complex. My local savior spoke briefly with the priest and gestured for me to wait.

After a few minutes, I was instructed to remove my shoes and summoned into the main hall to meet the head priest. Following a brief, incomprehensible discussion about my predicament, I was handed over to a man who appeared to be the church warden. He led me to a building on the far side of the temple grounds. Inside was a large, dry room containing two beds—the long-promised "hostel". He pointed out the communal water tap located around the back of the temple, which was accessible as long as I put my shoes back on.

Supper with a Scripture Student

Shortly afterward, my roommate for the night arrived. The room itself was dark and dingy, even with the single lightbulb switched on, and it looked rather dusty. However, it was dry, which was all that mattered as the rain continued to lash outside.

My roommate and I did our best to converse. I discovered he was a student learning temple scriptures, traveling from temple to temple, and was actually heading to Kaman the following day—the very town I needed to pass through. He kindly offered to share his humble supper of spicy rice and sauce. Lacking any utensils, he looked around briefly before using his bare hand to scoop a generous portion of the messy rice onto a spare plate, accompanied by a fresh chappati. It was incredibly delicious, and I suddenly realized I hadn't eaten a proper meal in hours.

The 4:00 AM Wake-Up Call

Around ten o'clock, we decided to call it a night. I made my string bed—which lacked a mattress—as comfortable as possible, wrapped myself in my sarong as a makeshift blanket, and tried to sleep.

Sleep, however, proved elusive. The night air was chilly, and the thin sarong offered little warmth, especially since I had been forced to change out of my rain-soaked clothes. I spent hours tossing and turning. Just as I finally managed to drift off, I was jolted awake by the deafening crackle of a nearby tannoy speaker system.

At exactly 4:00 AM, the local priest next door began his morning wailing call to prayer, intended to summon the village women to the temple. My roommate, who had been sleeping soundly, scrambled out of bed and spent ten frantic minutes searching the pitch-black room for a switch to turn off the speaker, but to no avail. We had no choice but to endure thirty minutes of high-volume wailing.

When the wailing finally ceased, the voices of the village women took over. It sounded as though the entire village had congregated right outside our door. While I couldn't discern their exact activities, there was a loud symphony of clashing metal pans and running water. By 6:00 AM, the clanging of temple bells and the rhythmic thumping of drums signaled the start of morning prayers. My roommate promptly left to join them, leaving me lying in the cold, listening to the relentless rain and dreading another day of getting soaked to the bone.

Reaching the Delhi-Agra Highway

Once the morning prayers concluded and the crowd dispersed, my roommate returned, cheerfully singing just as he had the night before. I got up, washed at the outdoor tap, and packed my gear. Before I departed, the priest kindly served me some hot, milky tea. It was exceptionally delicious, and I happily drank two glasses while answering his questions as best as I could.

I bid my hosts a warm farewell and paid the 50-rupee room charge. It was a modest fee, and I was immensely grateful for their hospitality in my hour of need.

After backtracking to Kaman, I finally joined the main Delhi-Agra highway. The rain had ceased, and the sun emerged, promising a hot day ahead. My bicycle and panniers were caked in a thick layer of dried mud and road grime from the previous day's downpour, and they were in desperate need of a deep clean.

The national highway was busy but a massive improvement over the secondary roads. It featured a clear white line designating a hard shoulder, which was heavily utilized by pedestrians, slow-moving vehicles, and local livestock. This separated lane made cycling significantly easier and safer, as the fast-moving traffic rarely crossed the white line.

Mathura: Holy Cows and Moldy Toast

The highway surface was smooth, allowing me to make rapid, efficient progress to Mathura. Arriving early in the day, I began searching for a hose to clean my mud-caked bicycle. I pulled into a small automotive repair shop equipped with a jet wash, but a sudden power cut brought the operation to a halt.

"Five minutes!" the elderly mechanic repeatedly promised. After sitting in silence for fifteen minutes, I decided to push on. The old man offered a polite, apologetic shrug as I departed.

Entering the city center of Mathura, I was surprised to find an incredibly dirty, scruffy, and chaotic environment. Mathura is celebrated as the birthplace of Lord Krishna, and a prominent temple marks the sacred site. The city was absolutely packed with pilgrims visiting the shrine.

I checked into a cheap, noisy hotel located on the very street of the main temple. Outside, the local cows ruled the roads, wandering freely and eating scraps of absolutely everything—with the notable exception of actual grass.

While the hotel room itself was tolerable, the meals were prepared by two young boys and were genuinely horrid. The next morning, I was served a breakfast of toast and jam. The toast arrived with distinct patches of green mold along the crusts. I carefully ate the mold-free center—which still tasted thoroughly unpleasant—and left the compromised edges on my plate.

Shortly after, the hotel manager strolled over for a friendly chat and inquired why I hadn't finished my meal.

I silently pointed to the green, fuzzy crusts.

He stared at the bread, looked at me, and then calmly picked up the moldy toast and ate it right in front of me to prove there was no issue.

Faced with that level of absolute, unwavering customer service defiance, there was simply nothing left to say.

Arrival in Agra

The final leg of my journey to Agra was a breeze. The 40-kilometre stretch was flat, smooth, and took very little time. The quality of the road made an immense difference; I resolved to stick strictly to national highways wherever possible.

Upon rolling into Agra, I finally spotted the signs pointing toward the legendary Taj Mahal—which, in true Indian fashion, was still another 12 kilometres away.

If I could do it all over again, I would:

  1. I found out much later that you can purchase local state maps from paper/book stalls. They are large scale and cheap
  2. If a priest gives a blessing - pay him!
  3. Consider sticking to the national highway where possible
