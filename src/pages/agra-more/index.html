Agra and More

Embark on a raw cycling adventure from Agra's Taj Mahal to the pink streets of Jaipur and Ranthambore. Read about wild tiger safaris and chaotic Indian highways

17 Aug 2026

I recently wrapped up a cycling trek across Rajasthan—from the misty, exhaust-choked streets of Agra to the wild jungles of Ranthambore.

Here is the completely honest, slightly bruised, and highly caffeinated travel blog of my journey. including the misty Taj Mahal, the stunning Palace of the Winds in Jaipur, the empty bird sancuary at Bharatpur and the raw wildlife of Ranthambore National Park

Agra: Monuments, Mirage, and... Pizza Hut

Agra is a city of two distinct personalities. On one hand, you have the main city: a sprawling, chaotic, and relentlessly busy Indian metropolis. On the other hand, you have the area surrounding the majestic Taj Mahal—still chaotic, yes, but tempered by cheap guesthouses, rooftop restaurants, and that unmistakable white marble dome dominating the skyline. ==This will have to be removed later==

While the standard recommendation is to stay right next to the Taj, our journey involved a hotel positioned about 1.5 kilometres away toward the city centre. This choice yielded an unexpected culinary triumph: a Pizza Hut just 200 metres down the road, which served up what was arguably one of the best meals of the trip—perfectly crispy pizzas, fresh salads, and fries.

When it came time to visit the Taj Mahal itself, the local transport sector was out in full force. Navigating a gauntlet of highly persistent tuk-tuk and rickshaw drivers requires a firm hand; after taking one to the monument, the driver insisted on waiting, which required an equally insistent refusal.

As for the monument itself? It looked like a glistening ghost, its pristine white marble shimmering through a hazy mist of environmental pollution to create a surreal, mirage-like effect. For tourists, entry is 750 rupees (a steep contrast to the fraction of the price paid by Indian nationals), and the grounds are packed with enthusiastic souvenir vendors and freelance photographers offering to capture your portrait against the Taj for a fee.

A subsequent visit to the Agra Fort (250 rupees) proved that while its massive red sandstone exterior is deeply impressive, the interior is somewhat of a letdown unless you are a passionate scholar of ancient ruins. The day, however, was salvaged beautifully by retreating to a "Lonely Planet-recommended" rooftop restaurant near the Taj to watch the sunset far above the crowds.

The Road to Bharatpur: Of Bureaucracy and Dry Wetlands

Escaping the gravity of Agra is no easy feat. The exit was misty, smelly, and incredibly disorienting, requiring frequent requests for directions while navigating the secondary road to Bharatpur. Along this 40-mile (64 km) stretch lies Fatehpur Sikri, home to an incredibly grand temple complex. While its exterior was imposing, the decision was made to keep pedalling rather than venture inside.

Upon arriving in Bharatpur, a hotel at the far end of town caught the eye, thanks to its vibrant, beautifully manicured garden. Clean, inexpensive, and offering an excellent breakfast, it provided a quiet sanctuary a comfortable 2 kilometres away from the noisy main town.

The main attraction here is the famous Bharatpur Bird Sanctuary. Entering the park, however, highlights the wonderfully eccentric side of local bureaucracy:

  • Human Entry Ticket: 200 rupees, issued in seconds by an automated machine.
  • Bicycle Entry Ticket: 25 rupees, which required an A5-size form to be meticulously filled out by hand, taking ten times longer to process than the human ticket.

Once past the gauntlet of guides and cycle-rickshaws, a 6-kilometre tarmac road leads through the heart of the reserve. Unfortunately, climate change has severely disrupted the monsoons, leaving the once-lush wetlands dry and dusty. The lack of water has triggered a dramatic decline in both bird populations and tourists.

What water did remain resembled something out of Mordor from The Lord of the Rings—thick, murky, and heavy with mud. Even so, the park still had its charms. Turtles with remarkably long necks and rather unattractive catfish patrolled the muddy pools, while waders, owls, and birds of prey dotted the branches. Venturing 20 miles (32 km) into the remote dirt tracks revealed sun-basking giant lizards and local women hard at work clearing reeds while the men watched from the shade.

By day two, a creeping suspicion arose. As the rickshaw guides pointed out the "wild" owls in the distant trees to their passengers, the birds appeared in the exact same positions as the previous day—leaving one to wonder if they had been discretely nailed to the branches for tourist convenience.

It was also here that I came across a fellow cycle tourist heading east toward Agra, complete with full camping gear. The brave soul explained his strategy of avoiding busy towns by pitching his tent in remote fields under the cover of darkness. However, he quickly discovered why camping is not a popular pastime in India: within fifteen minutes of pitching his tent, a curious head would pop through the flap with a cheerful, "Hello, how are you?", followed shortly by a gathering crowd of fascinated locals.

Jaipur and Tonk: Pink Palaces and Pricey Pedals

The ride to Jaipur was flat and straightforward, though a mid-route inspection of a scruffy, overpriced hotel in Dausa (51 km before Jaipur) delayed progress. Consequently, the outskirts of the "Pink City" were reached after nightfall, completely obscuring the unique buildings carved directly into the rocky hillsides along the entrance road.

Unfortunately, Jaipur did not quite live up to the nighttime anticipation. While the famous pink-hued structures—including the spectacular Palace of the Winds—were visually striking, from a cyclist's perspective, the city was not worth the detour. In hindsight, a direct secondary road from Bharatpur to Sawai Madhopur would have saved a day.

Instead, the route continued down the national highway to Tonk, an up-and-coming town undergoing a massive construction boom. The accommodation here was wonderfully bizarre: an isolated hotel located 3 kilometres down the highway, surrounded by barren land and a solitary pond, with the nearest building a full kilometre away.

During a leg-stretching stroll to the local Hero Honda dealership, I discovered that a brand-new motorcycle could be purchased for 38,000 rupees (roughly £500)—the exact same price as his touring bicycle. This revelation explained the bewildered looks from local onlookers: why on earth would a wealthy Westerner choose to expend immense physical effort pedalling across India when a perfectly good motorbike costs the exact same amount?

Ranthambore: The High-Stakes Tiger Chase

An easy, scenic ride along a quiet secondary road lined with vegetable fields led to Sawai Madhopur, the gateway to Ranthambore National Park.

A 6:30 AM safari (550 rupees) is an absolute must-do. The journey began in a chilly mountain breeze, with fifteen tourists bundled up in blankets and fleeces inside an open-air vehicle resembling an old military wagon.

It did not take long before the primary target was spotted, triggering an immediate, high-stakes vehicular chase. Five or six tourist vehicles bounded wildly over the rough, rocky mountain tracks, jockeying aggressively for the ultimate photo angle. The tiger, displaying a magnificent level of casual indifference, ignored the motorized chaos entirely and sauntered slowly into the reed beds.

Beside the big cats, the park was teeming with spotted deer, monkeys, diverse birdlife, and crocodiles masquerading as drifting logs in the lakes.

A second tiger came into view much closer to the tourist jeeps. Unbothered by the commotion of the bouncing vehicles and the tourists snapping photos, this tiger casually walked along the edge of a nearby reed bed.

The scene quickly intensified as the predator moved into the thicket:

The Ambush: The tiger disappeared into the reed bed, and almost immediately, the group heard the sudden cry of a young deer.

  • Cat and Mouse: The tiger had successfully caught the deer and seemed to be "playing" with its victim in the way a domestic cat does.
  • The Jungle's Alarm: While the struggle was hidden from sight, the surrounding jungle erupted. Adult deer and monkeys began making incredibly loud distress calls in an attempt to frighten the tiger into releasing its prey.
  • An Auditory Struggle: Although the tourists could not physically see what was happening behind the reeds, they could hear the heartbreaking cries of the young deer and the low growls of the tiger for what felt like a very long time.

It was a stark, thrilling, and poignant reminder of the wild, well worth the 4-hour safari and a generous 100-rupee tip for the guide and driver.

Kota to Mumbai: The Final Stretch and the Iron Road

The final cycling leg to Kota was a breeze, save for two notable events:

  1. The First Puncture: Courtesy of a long, iron-hard thorn left on the roadside by local firewood collectors.
  2. The Snake Incident: Running directly over a long brown snake at the very last second. Fortunately, the reptile slithered off into the grass entirely unharmed.

Having grown weary of the flat, repetitive agricultural scenery and eager to sample India's legendary railway system, the author decided to skip the remaining 7 to 8 days of riding to Mumbai and booked a train from Kota instead.

It promised to be "quite an experience"—and it certainly did not disappoint.
